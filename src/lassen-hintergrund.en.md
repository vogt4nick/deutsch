## Why *lassen* Means Both *let* and *make*

English divides the territory of *lassen* among five verbs: *let*, *leave*,
*make*, *have*, and *stop*. German uses one. This page looks at why that works,
and why the textbook label for *sich lassen* can mislead. For forms and rules,
see the [*lassen* reference](lassen.en.md). To pick the German for an English
sentence, see
[How to say *let*, *leave*, *make*, *have*, and *stop*](lassen-uebersetzen.en.md).

### One Core Idea

*Lassen* is related to English *let*, and its Indo-European root means roughly
*to slacken, to go limp*. Schuchart traces every meaning back to that single
idea: investing no energy.[^yourdailygerman_lassen1]

Seen that way, the meanings stop looking like a list and start looking like one
attitude seen from different sides. You *lass* a habit by putting no more energy
into it: *Sie hat das Rauchen gelassen* (she quit smoking). You *lass* a
situation by putting no energy into changing it: *Lass alles, wie es ist* (leave
everything as it is). You *lass* someone do something by putting no energy into
stopping them: *Lass mich rein* (let me in).

This is also why the first three meanings blur. *Ich lasse meine Haare wachsen*
(I'm growing my hair out) is both *leaving as is* and *allowing*, and nothing
depends on which you call it. The meanings are easy to learn. The hard parts are
the grammar and telling *lassen* apart from *verlassen*.

### Let or Make?

*Sie lässt die Kinder fernsehen* can mean she lets the children watch TV or that
she has them watch it. English must choose between *let* and *make*. German
doesn't, because *lassen* fits permission, command, and persuasion
alike.[^nadathur_lauer]

Lauer and Nadathur explain both readings with one meaning: the subject's action
is *sufficient* for the event to happen.[^lauer_nadathur] Whether that sounds
like permission or an order depends on what the listener takes for granted. If
the children want to watch TV anyway, the mother's action only removes an
obstacle, and we hear *let*. If they don't, her action is what makes it happen,
and we hear *make*.

So the ambiguity isn't a flaw in German. English forces a distinction that
German leaves to context.

### Is *sich lassen* a Passive?

Textbooks file *Die Tür lässt sich öffnen* under Passiversatzform, a substitute
for *Die Tür kann geöffnet werden*. The paraphrase works, but the label hides
what is going on.

Pitteroff shows that *sich lassen* behaves like a middle construction, the same
family as *Das Buch liest sich gut* (the book reads well).[^pitteroff] A middle
describes a property of the subject: this door is the kind that opens, this book
is the kind that reads well. That's why it pairs so naturally with *leicht*,
*schwer*, and *gut*.

Schuchart goes further: *sich lassen* is simply the ordinary *allow* meaning
plus a reflexive pronoun.[^yourdailygerman_lassen1] The door "allows itself" to
be opened. With an inanimate subject that sounds like possibility. With a cat
(*Die Katze lässt sich streicheln*) it sounds like permission again. Seen this
way, *sich lassen* needs no separate rule, only attention to the subject.

### Prefix Verbs and *verlassen*

Most prefix verbs keep the core ideas of *leaving as is* and *allowing* and only
add how or where.[^yourdailygerman_lassen2] *Anlassen* leaves something on,
*rauslassen* lets something out, *weglassen* leaves something out. The prefix
changes the direction, not the attitude.

*Verlassen* is the exception that matters. English *leave* means two opposite
things: to let something stay put, and to go away from it. German gives each
meaning its own verb. *Lassen* keeps the investing-no-energy sense: the keys
stay at home. *Verlassen* means going away from a place or person. That's why
*Ich habe meine Schlüssel zu Hause verlassen* sounds wrong to a German ear: it
says you walked out on your keys.
