## The Meanings of *lassen*

*Lassen* covers ground that English splits across several verbs: *let*, *leave*,
*make*, *have*, and *stop*. Schuchart traces every meaning back to one core
idea: investing no energy. The verb is related to English *let*, and its
Indo-European root means roughly *to slacken, to go
limp*.[^yourdailygerman_lassen1] The meanings are easy to learn. The hard parts
are the grammar and telling *lassen* apart from *verlassen*.

### Core Meanings

| Meaning        | Example                              | English                      |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------- |
| to abstain     | *Lass das!*                          | Stop that!                   |
| to leave as is | *Lass das Fenster offen.*            | Leave the window open.       |
| to allow       | *Lass mich rein.*                    | Let me in.                   |
| to cause       | *Ich lasse mein Fahrrad reparieren.* | I'm having my bike repaired. |
| to be possible | *Die Tür lässt sich öffnen.*         | The door can be opened.      |
| to suggest     | *Lass uns gehen.*                    | Let's go.                    |

The first three meanings blend into one another. *Sie hat das Rauchen gelassen*
means she quit smoking. *Lass alles, wie es ist* (leave everything as it is)
keeps things as they are. *Ich lasse meine Haare wachsen* (I'm growing my hair
out) sits between leaving as is and allowing. Schuchart notes that *Lass das!*
sounds sharper than *Lass es* (drop it): the first stops something in progress,
the second talks someone out of a plan.[^yourdailygerman_lassen1]

Without a second verb, *lassen* usually means abstaining or leaving as is. With
an infinitive, it usually means allowing or causing. As with the modal verbs,
the infinitive takes no *zu*: *Sie lässt mich gehen* (she lets me go).

### Allowing or Causing

With an infinitive, *lassen* is often ambiguous. *Sie lässt die Kinder
fernsehen* can mean she lets the children watch TV or has them watch it. English
has to choose between *let* and *make*; German covers both with one
verb.[^nadathur_lauer] Nadathur and Lauer explain both readings with a single
meaning: the subject's action is enough for the event to happen. Whether that
reads as permission or as an order depends on what is taken for granted in the
background, such as whether the other person wants the event
anyway.[^lauer_nadathur] Context usually settles it: *Die Eltern lassen die
Kinder länger aufbleiben* (the parents let the children stay up later) is
permission, *Die Lehrerin lässt die Klasse nachsitzen* (the teacher keeps the
class in detention) is an order. To be unambiguous, say *erlauben* or *dafür
sorgen, dass*.

The person doing the action is in the accusative: *Ich lasse ihn arbeiten.*
Leave that person out, and what remains is a service you arrange. This form is
very common and sounds neutral.[^yourdailygerman_lassen1]

| German                                | English                       |
| ------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| *Ich lasse mein Fahrrad reparieren.*  | I'm getting my bike repaired. |
| *Ich lasse mich massieren.*           | I'm getting a massage.        |
| *Ich lasse mich scheiden.*            | I'm getting a divorce.        |
| *Ich lasse mir die Haare schneiden.*  | I'm getting a haircut.        |
| *Ich lasse mir die Rechnung bringen.* | I'll ask for the bill.        |

The reflexive pronoun is accusative when you are the object of the action (*mich
massieren*) and dative when the sentence has another object (*mir die Haare
schneiden*). Name the person doing it with *von*: *Ich lasse mir die Haare von
meiner Schwester schneiden* (my sister cuts my hair).

### Possibility with *sich lassen*

With an inanimate subject, *sich lassen* says that something is possible.
Textbooks call this a Passiversatzform because it replaces a passive with
*können*.

| Passive with *können*             | *sich lassen*                     | English                  |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| *Die Tür kann geöffnet werden.*   | *Die Tür lässt sich öffnen.*      | The door opens.          |
| *Das Problem kann gelöst werden.* | *Das Problem lässt sich lösen.*   | The problem is solvable. |
| *Das kann leicht erklärt werden.* | *Das lässt sich leicht erklären.* | That's easy to explain.  |

The form describes a property of the subject, much like *Das Buch liest sich
gut* (the book reads well). Pitteroff therefore classes it as a middle
construction.[^pitteroff] It often appears with *nicht*, *leicht*, *schwer*, or
*gut*. The impersonal version is *Hier lässt es sich leben* (life is good
here).[^duden_lassen]

With an animate subject, permission comes back to the fore. *Die Katze lässt
sich streicheln* means the cat lets people pet it. *Ich lasse mich gern
überzeugen* means I'm open to persuasion. That is why Schuchart finds the term
Passiversatzform unhelpful: *sich lassen* is just the ordinary *allow* meaning
plus a reflexive pronoun, which reads as passive depending on the
subject.[^yourdailygerman_lassen1]

### Suggestions with *lass uns*

*Lass uns* corresponds to English *let's*. The form depends on who you're
talking to: *Lass uns gehen* (du), *Lasst uns gehen* (ihr), *Lassen Sie uns
gehen* (Sie).[^duden_lassen] It is less common in German than *let's* is in
English; people often say *Gehen wir!* instead. As Schuchart points out,
*lassen* itself can follow: *Lass es uns lassen* means *let's skip
it*.[^yourdailygerman_lassen1]

### Perfekt

The Präteritum is *ließ*, but in conversation the Perfekt is more common.
Without an infinitive, *lassen* forms the Perfekt with *gelassen*. With an
infinitive, it takes the Ersatzinfinitiv *lassen*, just like the modal
verbs.[^duden_lassen]

| Construction       | Perfekt                                     | English                 |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| without infinitive | *Ich habe den Schlüssel zu Hause gelassen.* | I left the key at home. |
| with infinitive    | *Ich habe dich schlafen lassen.*            | I let you sleep.        |
| with infinitive    | *Ich habe mir die Haare schneiden lassen.*  | I had my hair cut.      |

With *liegen*, *stehen*, and *fallen*, usage varies: *Ich habe das Glas fallen
lassen* and *fallen gelassen* (I dropped the glass) are both
common.[^yourdailygerman_lassen2] Duden lists *gelassen* after an infinitive as
the rarer variant.[^duden_lassen] Prefix verbs, by contrast, take a participle:
*Er hat das Seil runtergelassen* (he lowered the rope), but *Er hat das Seil
runterhängen lassen* (he let the rope hang down).

In a subordinate clause, the auxiliary goes in front of the two infinitives.

| Clause      | Example                                   |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| main        | *Ich habe sie schlafen lassen.*           |
| subordinate | *..., dass ich sie habe schlafen lassen.* |
| not         | *..., dass ich sie schlafen lassen habe.* |

The modal verbs follow the same order: *..., weil ich habe arbeiten müssen*
(because I had to work). In fast conversation, even native speakers trip over
it.[^yourdailygerman_lassen2]

### Prefix Verbs

Most prefix verbs keep the core ideas of *leaving as is* and *allowing* and only
add how or where.[^yourdailygerman_lassen2]

| Verb                       | Meaning                        | Example                               |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------- |
| *anlassen*, *auflassen*    | leave on, leave open           | *Lass das Radio an.*                  |
| *reinlassen*, *rauslassen* | let in, let out                | *Lass die Katze raus.*                |
| *weglassen*                | leave out                      | *Ich lasse die Butter weg.*           |
| *auslassen*                | skip                           | *Ich lasse das Frühstück aus.*        |
| *zulassen*                 | allow, approve                 | *Das Amt lässt den Impfstoff zu.*     |
| *überlassen*               | leave to, hand over            | *Das überlasse ich dir.*              |
| *hinterlassen*             | leave behind (message, estate) | *Hinterlassen Sie eine Nachricht.*    |
| *entlassen*                | lay off, release               | *Die Firma entlässt 200 Mitarbeiter.* |
| *erlassen*                 | enact; waive                   | *Das Parlament erlässt ein Gesetz.*   |
| *belassen*                 | formal for *leave as is*       | *Wir belassen es dabei.*              |
| *verlassen*                | leave (a place or person)      | *Er hat seine Frau verlassen.*        |

### *Verlassen*

English *to leave* has two meanings: to let something remain and to go away.
German splits them. *Lassen* means to let remain, *verlassen* means to go away.
*Verlassen* always needs an accusative object and sounds formal; Schuchart says
*Wir haben die Bar um zwei verlassen* sounds like a police
report.[^yourdailygerman_lassen2] In everyday speech, people say *Wir sind um
zwei aus der Bar gegangen* (we left the bar at two), and without an object *Ich
gehe jetzt* or *Ich mach mich auf den Weg* (I'm heading out).

*Sich verlassen auf* with the accusative means *to rely on*: *Du kannst dich auf
mich verlassen* (you can count on me). The adjectives *zuverlässig* and
*unzuverlässig* (reliable, unreliable) come from it.

### Translation Traps

| English                   | German                                        | Not                                            |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| *I'm leaving.*            | *Ich gehe jetzt.*                             | *Ich verlasse.*                                |
| *I left my keys at home.* | *Ich habe meine Schlüssel zu Hause gelassen.* | *Ich habe meine Schlüssel zu Hause verlassen.* |
| *You can rely on me.*     | *Du kannst dich auf mich verlassen.*          | *Du kannst auf mich verlassen.*                |
| *There's one beer left.*  | *Es ist noch ein Bier da.*                    | *Ein Bier ist gelassen.*                       |
| *I had my hair cut.*      | *Ich habe mir die Haare schneiden lassen.*    | *Ich hatte meine Haare geschnitten.*           |

*Ich hatte meine Haare geschnitten* means *I had cut my hair*, that is,
yourself. For *left* in the sense of remaining, German uses *noch*, *da*, or
*übrig*: *Ist noch Suppe übrig?* (is there any soup
left?)[^yourdailygerman_lassen2]

### Idioms

Many fixed expressions with *lassen* need a free translation.

| Expression                                         | English                          |
| -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| *Lass dir Zeit.*                                   | Take your time.                  |
| *Lass es lieber sein.*                             | Better skip it.                  |
| *Das lasse ich mir gefallen.*                      | I could get used to that.        |
| *Das kann sich sehen lassen.*                      | That's impressive.               |
| *Das lässt sich hören.*                            | That sounds reasonable.          |
| *Über Geschmack lässt sich nicht streiten.*        | There's no accounting for taste. |
| *Die Kritiken lassen auf einen guten Film hoffen.* | The reviews promise a good film. |
